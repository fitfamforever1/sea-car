# Faith, State and the Price of the Difference

### How enforced religion shapes education, science, women's participation, minority rights and public health in Pakistan

---

**What this is:** An examination of how religion, when enforced through law, politics and social pressure, affects education, science, women's participation, minority rights and public health in Pakistan. Every figure comes from a reputable source such as the World Bank, the World Economic Forum, Pew Research and rights organisations.

**What this isn't:** An attack on Islam, the Quran, Muhammad or anyone's personal faith. We separate faith, a privately held conviction, from the use of religion as an instrument of state and politics, and we critique only the second. Nothing here targets any sect, community or individual, and I don't hate any religion.

**What I don't claim:** That religion is the only cause of Pakistan's problems. Poverty, weak governance, military influence in politics, feudal land ownership and debt are also major factors.

---

## Introduction

One of the most revealing questions about any society is not how devout its people are, but what happens when doctrine and evidence collide, and who wins. If arithmetic says 2+2=4 and authority insists on 5, a country can absorb the cost for a while, but the bill compounds.

In Pakistan, religion is more than a private conviction. It is a political veto over curriculum, the courtroom, family law and what can be safely said. This essay indicts that arrangement, and the pattern across education, science, gender and health is consistent enough to demand an explanation.

## Education: The Empty Classroom

A nation builds its future in its classrooms, and Pakistan is barely filling them.

- **Literacy** among those aged ten and over is **63%**: **73%** for men against **54%** for women.
- **Out-of-school children:** the latest household survey puts **28%** of children out of school, about **20 million**. UNICEF estimates **25–26 million**, making Pakistan home to the second-largest out-of-school population in the world.
- **Spending:** education receives **1.9% of GDP**. Research gets **0.16%**, with **474 researchers per million people**.

Poverty explains a lot of this, but priorities explain the rest. For decades, it has been cheaper to signal piety than to build schools.

## Science and Ideas: How a Society Treats Its Best Minds

A society's relationship with ideas shows in how it treats its best minds.

- Abdus Salam won Pakistan's only Nobel Prize, in 1979. A 1974 constitutional amendment had already declared his community, the Ahmadis, non-Muslim. After his death, the word "Muslim" was scrubbed from his gravestone.
- In 2017, Mashal Khan, a student at a Pakistani university, was beaten to death by fellow students over a blasphemy accusation that investigators later found baseless.

When asking the wrong question can cost you your life, people stop asking questions. A country that stops asking questions stops inventing things.

## Blasphemy Laws: How It Works in Practice

- The harsher provisions were introduced by the military government in the early 1980s.
- In the first four years after the death penalty was added, there were no more than **18 cases a year**.
- At least **2,449 people** have been formally accused since 1987, and **95** were killed in extrajudicial attacks between 1994 and 2023.
- A record **344 new cases** were registered in 2024.
- Christians are under **2%** of the population but **6%** of those prosecuted.
- More than half of the accused are Muslims themselves, which means the law is used to settle scores more than to protect faith.
- In 2024, a tourist was lynched in Swat over an alleged insult to the Quran. A mob had earlier destroyed more than 20 churches.

## Women and Demographics: Half the Talent on the Bench

No country can leave half its talent on the bench and expect to compete.

- Pakistan ranked **third from the bottom** in the World Economic Forum's 2026 Global Gender Gap report.
- Female labour force participation is just **28%**, and only **8%** of women hold senior managerial roles.
- Women lack equal inheritance rights and face unequal rights in divorce.
- **29%** of girls are married before 18.
- When parliament moved to set a minimum marriage age of 18 in 2025, the Council of Islamic Ideology declared the bill un-Islamic, arguing that Islamic law does not recognise marital rape, even involving minors.
- Population growth is **2.55%** and the fertility rate is **3.6 children per woman**. Early marriage, limited schooling and low employment for women feed high fertility, and high fertility strains the schools and jobs the country can't provide.

## Public Health: Can a Society Follow Evidence?

Public health tests whether a society can follow evidence.

- Pakistan and Afghanistan are the only two countries where wild polio remains endemic, and militant groups have attacked vaccination campaigns.
- Pakistan recorded **74 cases in 2024** and **31 in 2025**.
- UN data shows **96 people** were killed in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa since 2012, including **27 health workers** and **61 police officers** guarding them.

The vaccine is the same one that cleared polio from most of the world. Where clerics and militants cast doubt on it, children are paralysed and the country becomes a global outlier.

## Public Support and Political Risk

None of this persists without public support.

- Pew's 2013 survey found that **84%** of Pakistani Muslims favour making sharia the official law, and about **three-quarters** backed the death penalty for leaving Islam.
- The data is dated, but it shows what politicians face.

Challenging a religious claim is treated as a career-ending risk, so reforms are judged first by whether they are Islamic and only then by whether they work. That is how a society ends up debating the minimum marriage age while its education system is failing.

## Conclusion

Faith can remain a deeply personal conviction. But when the state makes decisions that affect everyone's education, health, rights and opportunities, those decisions should be based on evidence, open to criticism and subject to public scrutiny.

Because when evidence says 4 and authority insists on 5, the important question isn't which belief feels more sacred. It's what the country pays for the difference.
